Personalized Newsletter Reference Architecture

Reference architecture

Personalized newsletter, in six nightly steps

A working design for an email that gives every reader a few stories chosen for them. Every box marked you supply is something you almost certainly already run — a CMS, an analytics warehouse, an email platform. Swap your own in; the shape of the system does not change.

The parts worth copying are the three operations in colour below, and the decision to do all of them on a schedule rather than while someone is waiting.

The three operations that carry the work

Everything else is plumbing you can write in an afternoon. These three are where the design decisions live.

Step 02 — embedding

Articles become vectors

Send each article's headline plus its one-line summary to an embedding model and store the vector it returns on the article row. Full body text dilutes toward generic; a headline alone is too thin and too clickbait-shaped.

This is the only place you call the model, and the only thing you ever send it is published article text. Reader data is never embedded — which keeps the privacy conversation short.

Step 04 — interest vector

A reader is the average of what they read

No model call. The reader's interest vector is derived arithmetically from the article vectors you already have:

  1. collect vectors of articles they opened
  2. subtract the corpus mean from each
  3. average them

Step 2 is audience dependent Every article from our newspaper that isn't wire copy shares a large common direction -- it's all some variety of "Arkansas" and "Local News." We factor out the "universal mean" from that out and it gave better results.

That may not be as significant if you're USA Today or the NYT. IDK how it would work for the Trace or the 19th News. TEST TEST TEST.

Step 05 — the match

Cosine similarity, once per night

For each reader, take the cosine similarity between their interest vector and every article in the candidate window, then keep the top few. One library call.

At ~20k readers against a few hundred articles this is a brute-force multiplication measured in milliseconds.

Learn from my mistakes

Each of these bit me -- and they're super obvious in retrospect.

01 One reader identifier, end to end

The id in your reading history has to be the same id your email platform puts in the request. Platforms usually carry two — an internal one and the one you assigned — and the template variable you reach for first is often the wrong one. Settle this before you write a schema, because it is the primary key of half your tables.

02 Ask for article ids, not URLs

If your reading history reports URLs and your CMS reports ids, you will be string-matching two systems that disagree.

03 Decide what happens when there is nothing to say

Some readers will have no history, and some nights something will fail. The endpoint must always return a complete, renderable lineup. We do this by making persoanlization an additive, not a replacement for our traditional morning email stories.

TEST, TEST, TEST

The best testing I was able to do was to do a holdback test. Synthetic data using only part of the reader's history, then run a sample pass and see if what you generated as personalized were articles the reader actually did wind up reading. Do that before you send anything.

It's always about buy-in

The biggest hurdles to get this into production aren't the tech -- that's easy or at least straightforward -- it's getting every department on board, making sure the newsroom is OK with what you're doing, and setting up A/B testing and reporting.
